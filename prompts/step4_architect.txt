Multiple analysis teams have produced a rich set of empirical results. Your job is to read everything, identify the most compelling story the data can tell through your particular analytical lens, and propose a specific paper map.

YOUR ANALYTICAL LENS: __LENS__

CRITICAL EVALUATION RULE: Judge findings by substantive importance and empirical credibility, not by how cheap or fast they were to produce. Do not downweight a stronger design because it was computationally heavy, and do not elevate a weaker convenience result because it was easier to run. If a stronger design is absent, distinguish between a true substantive limitation and an execution shortcut.

DISCARD DISCIPLINE: Be explicit about findings that are empirically real but should stay out of the paper's core claim set because they are too weak, too sample-specific, too identification-fragile, too mechanistically speculative, or too off-argument. Anything you place in `Findings to Discard` will be treated by later steps as presumptively excluded, especially from the Introduction, Discussion, and Conclusion.

Based on everything you've read, propose a paper. Write your proposal to `paper_map___N__.md` with these sections:

## Core Argument
State the paper's central claim in 3–4 sentences. This must be a substantive argument — what the paper *claims*, not what it *studies*. A reader should be able to disagree with this claim.

## Contribution
Why is this argument surprising, important, or at odds with what prior literature would predict? What does this paper change about how scholars understand the topic? Be specific about which prior findings or theoretical expectations this challenges.

## Identification Strategy
What is the primary empirical strategy for the paper you're proposing? How strong is the causal identification? What are the main threats to identification and how does the evidence address them? If you are proposing a different identification strategy than the one in findings_brief.md, explain why it is superior.

## Paper Map
For each section of the paper (Introduction, Background, Data, Methods, Results, Discussion, Conclusion, Appendix), write 3–5 sentences describing what it covers and which specific findings, figures, and tables it draws on. This should read as a detailed outline that a writing agent could follow.

## Central Findings (Body of Paper)
List the specific results, figures, and tables that belong in the main body. For each, state: (a) what it shows, with specific numbers from the log output and Quarto chunk results, and (b) why it is central to the argument — how it advances the claim.

## Supporting Findings (Appendix)
List results, figures, and tables that belong in the appendix (robustness checks, secondary heterogeneity, alternative specifications, supplementary analyses). For each, briefly note what it supports.

## Findings to Discard
List any results from the extensions or initial analyses that do not fit the proposed argument and should be dropped. Be concrete: name the result, the source table/figure/R script if possible, and whether it is being dropped because of weak identification, weak precision, sample misalignment, external-validity limits, or narrative irrelevance. Include tempting results that would sound good in a seminar but would overstate the paper if they resurfaced in the main text.

## Potential Weaknesses
What would a skeptical reviewer challenge about this argument and evidence? Be honest — identify the 2–3 most serious objections and how (or whether) the current evidence addresses them.
